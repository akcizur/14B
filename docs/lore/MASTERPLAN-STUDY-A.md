# Masterplan Study A — spatial alternatives

**Status:** Design study / proposal. Nothing in this document is approved canon or implementation geometry yet.  
**Purpose:** Resolve the habitat's overall spatial logic and the three-street layout before building new world geometry.

## 1. Non-negotiable constraints

- District 14 is a small artificial urban habitat in outer space.
- The urban layout contains **exactly three streets**: one dominant Main Street and two secondary streets.
- Main Street is an extremely long, continuous street with buildings lining **both sides**, extending toward the far horizon.
- The city develops primarily along the Main Street axis and vertically, not by multiplying parallel streets.
- The habitat is protected by a massive enclosure/ring structure. The exact geometry and engineering are open.
- The artificial atmosphere and lighting must feel like a controlled environment, not simply an Earth skybox.
- Architectural direction: Czechoslovak urban/residential character from roughly 1950–1975, with restrained retro-futurist technology.
- Buildings must be spatially plausible: façade/window locations, floor levels, rooms, staircases, roof, and exterior dimensions must agree. No furnishing work is required at this stage.

## 2. Habitat envelope: three options to compare

### A — Elongated capsule / stadium envelope (leading study)

An elongated enclosure with rounded or softened ends and a long central axis. Main Street follows the long axis. The envelope can be visually vast and partly obscured by atmospheric haze, structural ribs, service infrastructure, or distant silhouettes.

**Advantages**
- Reinforces the long street and a sense of distance.
- Allows a strong central perspective and gradual discovery.
- Can visually support vertical city layers without implying a circular road.

**Risks / unresolved**
- Could look like a conventional tunnel if the ceiling curvature is too visible or repeated.
- The end caps and structural rhythm need to be designed carefully.
- Does not by itself solve gravity, pressure, shielding, or atmosphere.

### B — Broad, irregular enclosure with a dominant urban spine

A large enclosed volume with a clearly dominant Main Street but less perfectly symmetrical geometry. The habitat may have widening/narrowing zones, large voids, and asymmetrical structural areas.

**Advantages**
- Feels less like a clean tube and more like a place with accumulated urban history.
- Gives room for landmarks and distinct street character.
- Allows visual asymmetry and non-uniform distances.

**Risks / unresolved**
- Harder to establish a coherent structural language.
- May become visually arbitrary without a disciplined masterplan.
- Requires a stronger skyline and scale strategy.

### C — Segmented industrial enclosure

The habitat is divided into large structural sections or bays. Segments are visible as engineering infrastructure, but the city must read as one continuous space rather than a set of disconnected game levels.

**Advantages**
- Strong industrial identity and believable maintenance infrastructure.
- Can support visual variation and clear construction logic.
- Makes it possible to frame distant views through structural openings or layered depth.

**Risks / unresolved**
- Segmentation could become repetitive or suggest short artificial chunks.
- Risk of turning the environment into a corridor of repeated frames.
- Segment length must not be chosen before visual testing.

### Study recommendation

**Option A is the first shape worth testing, not a final decision.** It best supports the long Main Street requirement. Option B should remain a serious alternative if the capsule begins to feel too tunnel-like. Option C may be combined with either envelope as a structural language rather than used as the entire habitat form.

## 3. Street topology: exactly three streets

All diagrams are conceptual, not to scale. Main Street is the long axis. The secondary streets are short branches and must not become parallel avenues.

### Topology 01 — Staggered, alternating branches (recommended study)

```text
                         STREET 02
                             |
                             |
Main Street  ================================>
                             |
                             |
                         STREET 03
```

The diagram is symbolic: branches should be staggered longitudinally and may sit on different sides of Main Street. They should have distinct lengths, widths, and destinations. Avoid a symmetrical crossroad pattern.

**Strength:** immediately readable hierarchy, no grid, and opportunities for distinct local places.

**Risk:** branch intersections may feel like a repeated game-layout formula if placed too regularly.

### Topology 02 — Both branches on one side

```text
Main Street  =================================>
                   |                 |
                   |                 |
               STREET 02        STREET 03
```

Two secondary streets leave Main Street from the same side, at different distances, and should not be aligned as parallel roads.

**Strength:** keeps the far side of Main Street more continuous and gives one-sided asymmetry.

**Risk:** can feel like a comb or service-road layout unless the branches have very different functions and forms.

### Topology 03 — One close branch, one distant branch

```text
          STREET 02
              |
Main Street  ================================>
                                         |
                                         |
                                     STREET 03
```

One branch sits relatively close to the initial playable area and the other appears much farther along Main Street.

**Strength:** gives a strong sense of discovery and progressive expansion.

**Risk:** the near branch could dominate the early area, while the distant one may feel disconnected until the world is developed.

### Recommendation for topology

Start with **Topology 01**, but keep intersections irregular and meaningful. Streets 02 and 03 should have different roles and end conditions. They may terminate in a public space, a local destination, or a habitat service zone, but these destinations are not decided yet. Pedestrian passages, courtyards, stairs, roof paths, and internal building corridors do not automatically count as additional streets; their role must be clearly distinguished in the design.

## 4. Main Street — initial cross-section principles

Do not finalize numerical widths yet. The street should be tested as a single readable composition:

- central road / vehicle zone;
- sidewalks on both sides, with pedestrian continuity;
- entrances and setbacks that vary subtly rather than forming a perfect wall;
- a consistent ground-floor rhythm, but varied building heights and rooflines;
- clear window and entrance scale;
- usable vertical clearance for aerial traffic, separate from pedestrian and ground traffic;
- visual access to the habitat enclosure at selected viewpoints, not a constant full reveal.

The existing provisional road and sidewalk dimensions are not approved. Any cross-section must be validated against actual buildings, vehicle scale, collision, walking space, and camera views.

## 5. Vertical profile and skyline

The city should not simply stack identical floors indefinitely. A proposed hierarchy:

1. **Street level:** familiar residential entrances, shops/services where appropriate, street furniture, trees or planting only where the environment plausibly supports them.
2. **Mid-height:** balconies, loggias, roof additions, bridges, occasional elevated infrastructure, varied residential silhouettes.
3. **Roof layer:** accessible roofs only where architecturally plausible; service equipment, vents, maintenance routes, occasional roof-to-roof connections.
4. **Upper airspace:** a restrained flight/hover layer with strong height and clearance rules. It must not visually or physically collide with roofs, façades, or ring infrastructure.

These are layers of spatial character, not promises that every building has every feature.

## 6. Building-height bands: qualitative, not dimensioned

Until the enclosure cross-section is chosen, use relative bands only:

- **Low:** local/special buildings that create breathing room and views.
- **Standard:** dominant housing blocks, aligned to the reference architectural family.
- **Tall:** rare vertical landmarks or housing clusters, used sparingly.
- **Roof structures:** additions, technical volumes, stair extensions, and equipment. They must not be mistaken for extra full residential floors.

A height rhythm should be gradual and locally varied, avoiding a uniform wall or a random skyline.

## 7. Sightline and enclosure visibility

Test at least four views before accepting a layout:

- eye level at the start of Main Street;
- eye level at a middle-distance intersection;
- rooftop view looking along the street;
- aerial/hover view looking down and toward the habitat shell.

The enclosure should be revealed selectively. Street level may show atmospheric haze, distant structure, or only a subtle curve; a roof or aerial viewpoint can reveal more. Avoid making every frame a giant obvious dome shot. Do not hide the fact that this is a habitat, but pace the reveal.

## 8. Atmosphere and light: first visual test set

Test three lighting moods before choosing a permanent palette:

- **Soft artificial daylight:** calm, diffuse, slightly muted, readable architecture.
- **Overcast habitat sky:** broad diffuse light, long-distance haze, gentle shadows.
- **Late artificial afternoon:** warm windows and localized lights against a cooler ambient environment.

The environment should not be perpetually dark cyberpunk. Neon is accent lighting, not the primary illumination. Any apparent sun, sky source, or day/night cycle remains an artistic proposal until the habitat's lighting mechanism is defined.

## 9. Architectural character zones along Main Street

Use a continuous street with gradual transitions, not hard 36 m chunks or obvious repeated level segments. The following is a **role study**, not a final sequence:

- **Residential stretch:** ordinary housing, entrances, balconies/loggias, trees or small planting pockets where appropriate.
- **Local node:** a denser area with services, gathering space, signs, and a wider pedestrian zone.
- **Landmark stretch:** one distinctive building, junction, façade, or infrastructure feature that breaks the rhythm.
- **Quiet / service edge:** utilities, maintenance access, less active façades, and a transition to another habitat function.

The exact order, lengths, and destinations remain open. Buildings should not repeat mechanically.

## 10. Initial exploration sequence

The player's first impressions should be layered:

1. At street level, the place reads as a familiar residential street with a coherent architectural scale.
2. Small anomalies reveal the artificial habitat: unusual distance, controlled light, structural details, signage, atmospheric haze.
3. From a wider intersection or rooftop, the player begins to see the enclosure's scale and vertical infrastructure.
4. From an aerial view, the three-street hierarchy and habitat boundary become more legible.

This is a suggested visual storytelling sequence, not a gameplay script.

## 11. Open decisions

- Habitat envelope: A, B, C, or hybrid?
- Cross-section and gravity model: still undefined.
- Street 02 and 03: which side, relative lengths, destinations, and distinct identities?
- Main Street: final cross-section, height rhythm, and pedestrian/vehicle separation?
- What structural elements are visible from street, roof, and aerial views?
- What are the lighting source and artificial-sky mechanism?
- Which architectural subtypes are represented and how are they mixed?
- Where can aerial vehicles travel and how is safe clearance enforced?
- What is the first approved playable section and its boundaries?

## 12. Design gate: first playable segment

A first segment is not ready for implementation until the design includes:

- an approved top-down masterplan sketch with exactly three streets;
- an approved habitat outline and cross-section study;
- a Main Street cross-section with grounded dimensions or clearly marked provisional values;
- one building prototype with consistent exterior/interior/floor/stair/window/roof dimensions;
- a defined pedestrian route and vehicle clearance envelope;
- selected sightlines showing enclosure visibility;
- a list of unresolved decisions explicitly kept out of implementation.
